2nd-5th century CE, exact date unknown. First Chinese Translation 5th century CE. &#8220;The Diamond That cuts through illusion&#8221; or the [Vajra][1] Cutter Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra&#8221; (Vajra &#8211; thunderbolt, diamond, adamantine weapon). This is a key [Mahāyāna][2] Sutra from the _[Prajñāpāramitā][3]_ family of texts.

Very popular in Chinese tradition as: _金剛般若波羅蜜多經_, _Jīngāng Bōrě-bōluómìduō Jīng_; shortened to 金剛經, _Jīngāng Jīng_.

First Chinese translation is thought to have been made by Kumarajiva (鳩摩羅什, J_iūmóluóshí_) : <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva>. Also translated by <a href="https://www.gaiaforest.org/tenzin/2024/10/24/xuanzang-%e7%8e%84%e5%a5%98/" data-type="post" data-id="2469">Xuanzhang</a>.

From Wikipedia

<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow">
  <p>
    The <em><strong>Diamond Sutra</strong></em> (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit">Sanskrit</a>: <em>Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra</em>) is a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana">Mahāyāna</a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism">Buddhist</a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutra">sutra</a> (a kind of holy scripture) from the genre of <em><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prajnaparamita">Prajñāpāramitā</a></em> (&#8216;perfection of wisdom&#8217;) sutras. Translated into a variety of languages over a broad geographic range, the <em>Diamond Sūtra</em> is one of the most influential Mahayana sutras in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia">East Asia</a>, and it is particularly prominent within the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Buddhism">Chan</a> (or <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen">Zen</a>) tradition,<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Schopen-1">[1]</a></sup> along with the <em><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_Sutra">Heart Sutra</a></em>&#8230;
  </p>
  
  <p>
    The <em>Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sutra</em> contains the discourse of the Buddha to a senior monk, Subhuti.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup> Its major themes are <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatman">anatman</a> (not-self), the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C5%ABnyat%C4%81">emptiness</a> of all phenomena (though the term &#8216;śūnyatā&#8217; itself does not appear in the text),<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Kalupahana,_David_J_page_156-13">[13]</a></sup> the liberation of all beings without attachment and the importance of spreading and teaching the <em>Diamond Sūtra</em> itself. In his commentary on the <em>Diamond Sūtra</em>, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hsing_Yun">Hsing Yun</a> describes the four main points from the sūtra as giving without attachment to self, liberating beings without notions of self and other, living without attachment, and cultivating without attainment.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Hsingyun-14">[14]</a></sup> According to Shigenori Nagatomo, the major goal of the <em>Diamond Sūtra</em> is: &#8220;an existential project aiming at achieving and embodying a non-discriminatory basis for knowledge&#8221; or &#8220;the emancipation from the fundamental ignorance of not knowing how to experience reality as it is&#8221;.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Shigenori-15">[15]</a></sup>
  </p>
  
  <p>
    In the sūtra, the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha">Buddha</a> has finished his daily walk to <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sravasti">Sravasti</a> with the monks to gather offerings of food, and he sits down to rest. Elder <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subhuti">Subhūti</a> comes forth and asks the Buddha: &#8220;How, Lord, should one who has set out on the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva">bodhisattva</a> path take his stand, how should he proceed, how should he control the mind?&#8221;<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Harrison-16">[16]</a></sup> What follows is a dialogue regarding the nature of the &#8220;perfection of insight&#8221; (<em>Prajñāpāramitā</em>) and the nature of ultimate reality (which is illusory and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shunyata">empty</a>). The Buddha begins by answering Subhuti by stating that he will bring all living beings to final <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana">nirvana</a>, but that after this &#8220;no living being whatsoever has been brought to extinction&#8221;.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra#cite_note-Harrison-16">[16]</a></sup> This is because a bodhisattva does not see beings through reified concepts such as &#8220;person&#8221;, &#8220;soul&#8221; or &#8220;self&#8221;, but sees them through the lens of perfect understanding, as empty of inherent, unchanging self.
  </p>

Frontispiece, Diamond Sutra from Cave 17, Dunhuang, ink on paper. A page from the [Diamond Sutra][4], printed in the 9th year of [Xiantong Era][5] of the [Tang dynasty][6], i.e. 868 CE. Currently located in the [British Library][7], London. This sutra was discovered at the [Mogao Caves][8] in [Dunhuang][9], but probably printed in [Sichuan][10] (see [&#8220;Printed dated copy of the _Diamond Sutra_&#8220;][11]). According to the British Library, it is “the earliest complete survival of a dated printed book”.
