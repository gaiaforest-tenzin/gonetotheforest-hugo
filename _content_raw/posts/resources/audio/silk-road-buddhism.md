This Dharma talk was offered at Gaia Forest's first working meditation weekend,  October 2026.  In this talk I trace some of Buddhism's historical journey along the Silk Road — from Gandhāran Buddha images with Greek aesthetics, through the Chinese practice of *géyì* (matching concepts across traditions), to the Yogācāra understanding of mind and its surprising convergences with Stoic philosophy, predictive-processing cognitive science, and the Christian contemplative tradition.

Beginning in this way with a brief chant in Pali feels very appropriate to me, in that one of the threads I hear in our conversation — and something we've touched on already this weekend — is this feeling of a crossroads, or a melting pot, of Buddhadharma in our time and place. But amongst contrast, maybe also finding a lot of confluence and compatibility.

From my own side, this has also been something I've very often — I wouldn't say discovered, more had pointed out to me by skilful teachers. The ones I appreciate most, of the ones who have encouraged — Thich Nhat Hanh among them — encouraged a kind of synthetic approach rather than a schismatic approach, recognising that there's a real importance in authenticity of lineage and transmission, but that also each of us is in some way like a Silk Road traveller in the Dharma. In some ways this image is a kind of embodiment of what I'll be talking about briefly — in the sense that actually, in the moment, or moments, after what we would call the Axial Age, when there was a huge ground-shift in civilisation in the Mediterranean region — what we know as kind of the main roots of our own culture, in Babylon, Egypt, Greece, the Celtic world — experienced an enormous upheaval, a shift from tribal and agrarian to cosmopolitan and metropolitan kinds of existence. A tension between city-states, a time of great uncertainty, that also gave birth to many of our most important spiritual traditions. That period of time stretches right across the origins of Buddhism, also of Christianity, and even leads us, though at a later date, to Islam. A kind of upturning of the status quo, and a beginning of deep questioning and an effort to understand the place of self in the world.

Very interestingly, around this same time is also the period of alphabetisation — a time when language and idea that had, up until that point, been oral, began to be written down, and grammar — especially subject and object — worked its way into our minds in a structural way that perhaps had not been the case before. Of course, we cannot know this for sure, but processes that had been going on since much earlier, in the late Palaeolithic — even the process of art and painting, the creation of image and idea, expression of feeling into artefact — led eventually to this amazing change that we are still living in to this day. It's a change that lies right at the heart of some of what we are experiencing as disconnection. In some important way, maybe the question we are asking is: how does our grammar of subject and object — how has it contributed not just to our worldview, but actually to the world we are now living in? How have we created a world predicated on a subject-object split? Right back to that first act of an ancestral human being taking up an object — a tool — and projecting it into the world. A kind of maybe unfortunate but very apt metaphor: to strike, maybe to kill, to survive. So how much influence has this split had on our way of being, and what is playing out in the world — something I think we can explore a little.

The reason for this is that, actually, in the centuries just after the life of the Buddha, Buddhism was in a process of travelling along the Silk Road, connecting North India — the home of the Buddha during his lifetime, where he taught in the Māgadhī language, which was later translated into Pali and eventually transmitted to Sri Lanka, to Burma. There were many different languages in which there was curiosity about these teachings, and they needed to be translated, but there were already existing ideas, indigenous ideas and ways of understanding, that were meeting these new ways of thinking and seeing. We have records of Greek philosophers meeting what they called the gymnosophists — which translates roughly as "the naked philosophers" — Jaina monks in Pakistan and present-day India, taking those ideas back to Greece. We also have the meeting between Taoist understanding — the teachings of Laozi — and Buddhist understanding arriving in China. They actually came up with a beautiful term in Chinese, *géyì*, which is the word for the noticing that they were matching ideas in these two traditions: that when you are contemplating something like emptiness — *śūnyatā* in Sanskrit and Pali — there is already a word, *wú*, in the Chinese, which is the underlying unmanifest that gives rise to yin and yang in the Taoist understanding. What we practise in the empty moment between out-breath and in-breath is a manifestation of *wú* in our experience, which can then give rise to an upward movement — a yang movement — and also a downward movement — a yin movement. So there was a pre-existing term, not pretending that they are identical, but noticing similarity, and harvesting the fruit of that friction between two different, somehow contrasting but also similar, ideas. Not just translation, but also new understanding coming from juxtaposition.

In some important way I feel like we are also kind of Silk Road travellers, as I mentioned, because we are at a time when we have a pre-existing cultural bedrock with, in many ways, very complete and beautiful and liberating understandings — for example the Judeo-Christian tradition, and in fact the Catholic tradition, the Catholic contemplative tradition especially, is what has offered us many of our English terms for Buddhist practice. A kind of extreme, and there are all kinds of other repercussions for the Western-science-based, or Western-philosophical, kind of bedrock. Newton also did something similar, arriving at very, very powerful equations for understanding the world in terms of inert objects that only move when they are struck by another — an amazing idea that allowed him to calculate the trajectories of the planets, but actually it's very different from the idea of continual, unmanifest upwelling of change from the unmanifest, which you find in Taoist understanding, and eventually also in the Yogācāra understanding, which is kind of the philosophy underpinning the Zen Buddhist tradition — Chan, Zen, and Plum Village. If we are stuck in the idea of inert objects outside, that need to be moved and manipulated — the way our language works — it's very difficult to deal with change and development, because here's this object out there, and here, inside, that actually is not staying the same, that is continually shifting and changing.

Plato has that beautiful image of the cave — who knows the story of Plato's cave? Imagining that our experience of the world is like we're all seated, actually chained, in a cave facing the wall.
