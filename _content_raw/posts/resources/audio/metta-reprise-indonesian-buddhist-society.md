Good morning, dear friends. Thank you for the opportunity to come and sit together with you in this beautiful space, next to this beautiful Buddha image you have here. Thank you also to Shin Shin for helping to organise my visit — Shin Shin has been very busy this week with beautiful musical offerings in the Mettā Centre community, including a performance from our friend Julian, who is also here today.

Last night, in the musical offerings, we had the opportunity to hear chanting in Chinese of Mahāyāna sūtras — the Heart Sūtra, a composition and rendition of the Mettā Sutta, a Pāli sutta — as well as other offerings inspired by the Christian tradition, from the European classical canon, and even contemporary Australian music. Thanks to Julian for that, and to the other musicians who shared classical and contemporary Australian pieces — music from our own time and place.

I'm reminded of one of my main meditation teachers' invitation: to value the essence of the Buddha's teachings, to study and practise authentically, but also to recognise the amazing diversity of the Buddha's teachings — so many different transmissions and skilful means.

I'm reminded, too, that while the image of Śākyamuni Buddha feels unmistakable to us — we feel we'll always recognise the Buddha, and feel a connection to the suttas, the original texts from more than two and a half thousand years ago in India — what we may not realise is that for more than two hundred years after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, there was no Buddha statue in the temple. Just an empty chair, a footprint, a wheel, or a caitya, a stūpa — this aniconic phase of the Dharma.

When the Buddha image first began to be carved in stone, it owed a great deal to the Greek tradition of sculpture: the curled hairstyle, the way the robes are folded on the body, is influenced by the image of the Greek god Apollo. In certain statues you find the contrapposto stance — the way Michelangelo's David also stands — a posture from Greek classical sculpture that you can see in Buddha images from this period.

The reason is that this type of Buddhist art arose in parts of the subcontinent — what is now Pakistan, Afghanistan, Kashmir — along the Silk Road, the great trade route connecting India, China and the West through Persia and eventually Greece. Following that route alongside the merchants, we find travelling monks and translators moving in both directions: figures like Xuanzang, whom some of you may know from *Journey to the West*, or *Monkey Magic* — great pilgrims, scholars and travellers carrying Buddha Dharma to new places, times, cultures and languages.

As I learned while preparing this talk, the Silk Road also had an important sea route — not just by land. Indonesia is central to this story: the transmission of Buddha Dharma through the Sunda Strait and the Strait of Malacca was very important, and perhaps as significant a figure as Xuanzang is the Tang-dynasty Chinese monk Yijing, who passed through Indonesia and spent a long time there.

This was around the seventh century. Yijing was travelling to Nālandā intending to study Buddha Dharma, but found Śrīvijaya such an important centre for Buddha Dharma at that time that he recommended other Chinese monks stop there to study Sanskrit and the Vinaya before continuing on to Nālandā. I felt a special connection hearing this, because my own teacher — who studied at the new Nālandā University — recommended that I also learn some Sanskrit to understand the Buddha Dharma more deeply. I'm ordained in the Sakya Tibetan lineage, and one of our patriarchs also studied at Nālandā; His Holiness the Dalai Lama says the Tibetan tradition is the Nālandā tradition, the North Indian tradition — the same one Yijing recommended Chinese monks prepare for by studying first in Śrīvijaya.

It's important to recognise that we too live at a crossroads — a time of exchange between cultures and languages. In Australia, we're living in a time rather like these seventh-century travellers and traders on the Silk Road, whether by land or by sea — except that we might call it not Silk Road Dharma but *Silicon* Road Dharma. We can look up a Dharma talk from any tradition, in any language, any time we like — even at one or two in the morning when we can't sleep. A wonderful thing, but also, at times, confusing: so many possibilities, so much to integrate into our practice, on the cushion and in daily life.

I want to talk a little about how this process of translating — not just words, but concepts and practices — happened in those times, and what we might learn for our own. How do we translate Dharma for our practice, our community, our time and place?

The first way I'll illustrate from the Chinese tradition. One of the earliest translation strategies is what's called "matching concepts" — *géyì* in Chinese. It's when I already have an idea and a word in my own language, and I meet a new idea and word from the Buddha Dharma, and say: these two more or less match, they're the same. In the Chinese tradition, the Taoists already had a concept of emptiness that seemed important, and when they encountered *śūnyatā*, they assumed it must be the same idea — in part because they believed the Buddha was actually Laozi, the patriarch of Taoism, returned to teach through his disciples. When we look more deeply, though, we find the concepts aren't necessarily identical. I won't unpack emptiness fully today — maybe another time, or in questions — but I want to move to the next stage.

After matching concepts, translators learned to be more careful, and this is where real translation begins: explaining what the new word means in the language of the time and place, while taking care to understand the true meaning behind it. So we get the Chinese character *kōng* (空) to translate *śūnyatā* — the character is literally a picture of an empty room, so you can really understand: there's nothing there from its own side. That begins to penetrate the understanding of *śūnyatā*.

Here's another example: *karuṇā*, compassion, in Sanskrit and Pāli. When I asked how to say it in Indonesian, I learned it is simply *karuṇā* too — the same word, used directly, since Sanskrit loanwords run deep in Indonesian. In English we use "compassion," which comes by way of the Christian — especially the Christian mystical — tradition: *com-* (with) + *passio*, from the Greek *pathos*, suffering — to suffer with, to resonate with another's pain. That seems to match *karuṇā* well enough, but going deeper, the *kar-* in *karuṇā* shares a root with *karma* — action. *Karuṇā* is active compassion: opening the heart to another's suffering without turning away, but — because it's joined with the wisdom of the cause and end of suffering — able to offer a hand in support rather than simply drowning in the same ocean of worldly grief (the near enemy of compassion, as distinct from its far enemy, cruelty).

That's a good example of the next phase: beyond matching concepts, we learn what's similar but also what's different — and as practitioners we become familiar enough with a term that we no longer need the English gloss; we can return to the original Pāli or Sanskrit and understand what it actually points to.

With that in mind, I'd like to take an important practice for our times as a further example of this crossroads, drawing also on the Christian tradition and on cognitive science — because that too is part of our Silk Road: we have access not only to the Buddha Dharma but to our own cultural roots and the different influences present in Australia. The Christian tradition has beautiful things to say about love and loving-kindness, so — after compassion, the second of the brahmavihāras — I'd like us to talk about mettā.

*Mettā* — "loving-kindness" is the usual English translation. When I asked about the Indonesian word, I learned the term used is *cinta kasih* — "loving affection" — and that *mettā*/*maitrī* is itself a very old word, related in the Vedas to Mitra, the god of alliance and covenant-bond (a word that survives directly in Indonesian as *mitra*, "ally" or "partner," also borrowed from Sanskrit). The ancient Indo-Aryan peoples — nomadic, often meeting new tribes and needing to forge strong bonds for survival — made offerings to Mitra to ensure that alliances would hold. *Mettā* is the Pāli form of *Mitra*. When the Buddha taught, he recognised that one of the conditions we need for *śamatha* is safety — freedom from fear.

One of my main teachers, very much a Silk Road Buddhist in his own practice — a scholar of Sanskrit and Chinese, trained in Theravāda, Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna — describes love as perhaps the most beautiful feeling a human being can have. When do we feel it? Usually face to face with someone beloved — our own flesh and blood, our kin. In English, this is where we get the word "kindness": the spontaneous way we relate to our own kin, our tribe, our village — the reason we're alive at all, because our ancestors felt this when they looked at us as children.

Something similar happened in the Christian tradition. When the New Testament was first rendered into Greek from Hebrew, the translators needed a word for *ahavah* — not only is love the most beautiful thing we can feel face to face with our beloved, but in this tradition, God is that feeling. They needed to translate "God is love" from Hebrew into Greek, and — because Greek already has several words for love — the word they settled on was *agápē*: not a worldly love between lovers, but a divine, creative love, the force that brought the world and human beings into being.

The four brahmavihāras — *mettā*, *karuṇā*, *muditā*, *upekkhā* — are also called "divine abodes." Why divine? Is it because this love is somehow out of reach, available only to angels or realised beings — or because it can be cultivated? The Buddha's own teaching is that *mettā* is a brahmavihāra because it is immeasurable, *appamāṇa* — the other name for these four qualities. We break through the usual limit on love, the one that normally confines it to flowing between ourselves and our own kin, and in that way it becomes immeasurable.

Bringing in some Western science: it's true that we're alive because of *mettā* — because our family cared for us unconditionally as infants; that love, in a sense, is what makes us human, and it has something of the same creative quality as *agápē*. But Western science also shows that the same process that lets us bond so strongly with family also helps us identify "the other" — whom not to trust. That's the tragedy built into this kind of bond: it creates an in-group and an out-group, and we can see in the world today what happens when that bond is very strong — real beauty, but also the real risk of making enemies of those we don't recognise or trust.

This is why *mettā* practice begins with ourselves, then moves to an easy object — a dear one, towards whom love flows spontaneously — before working up to a more challenging object: someone we recognise but have no strong connection to, training ourselves to feel that they too are worthy of kindness, worthy of freedom from suffering, because like us, they suffer. Eventually we explore extending that same kinship even to a difficult being — someone ordinarily outside the circle of our love. Loving-kindness doesn't just make us human through the love we receive from our parents; it's also capable of turning an enemy into a friend, a stranger into kin. In the Mahāyāna, *mettā* can be cultivated towards any phenomenon at all, transforming not just how we relate to beings but to the whole of our experience.

This is why the Buddha offered loving-kindness meditation not only as the fruit of the path, the quality of mind of an awakened being, but also right at the beginning — alongside *ānāpāna* practice, *mettā* can help us make friends even with our own discomfort, or a painful conversation earlier in the day that might otherwise get in the way of meditation. It's a beautiful practice for the beginning, the middle, and the end of the path.

So — out of respect for the custom of finishing the Dharma talk before midday — let me conclude for now. We live at a crossroads: of information about the Dharma, of culture and language, often speaking more than one language and holding more than one spiritual tradition within a single life. We can be inspired by a Silk Road approach to the Dharma, as long as we're not satisfied with merely matching concepts, but look deeper into the roots of our terms — and stay open, in the spirit of Indonesia's own national motto: not difference dividing us, but diversity uniting us. We can also draw on Western psychology or the Christian tradition to go deeper in practice, and to work through daily difficulties — starting with making friends with ourselves. This afternoon, I'd like to share a guided meditation on exactly that: we'll do some *mettā bhāvanā* after lunch.
