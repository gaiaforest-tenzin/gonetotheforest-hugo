It is perhaps not widely recognised that the wavy ringlets and folded robes of an early Buddha statue owe as much to the aesthetics of Ancient Greece as to those of India. Such images appeared centuries after the Buddha's passing, in a time of great upheaval but also exchange — along the Silk Road. Linking China to the Mediterranean through Buddhist kingdoms in what is now Afghanistan, Pakistan and Kashmir, this trade network carried not only silk and art but ways of thinking, including Buddhism. Translators such as Xuanzang and Kumārajīva worked to render these ideas into Chinese language and into practices relevant to a new time and place. This talk explores what a "Silk Road Dharma" perspective looks like in our own time, taking loving kindness (mettā) meditation as the example.

A very warm welcome, dear friends, greetings once again. Thank you again to the Buddhist Library for this opportunity to speak about a topic that feels alive for me at the moment — one I found myself reflecting on arriving here, coming to Glebe of all places to find this rich repository of Dharma books, and a group of good people such as yourselves, interested in the Dharma. Far as we are from Patna, not so far from Petty — we're here in a very new time and place for the Dharma in some important ways, and the process, as practitioners, of navigating the many different instructions it's possible to find as Buddhists in this day and age — different doctrines, different schools — is a great opportunity, a great privilege, but also sometimes a challenge. That's the inspiration behind this idea of a Silk Road Dharma, and especially "kinship at the crossroads." I hope to do a reasonable job of explaining what I mean by that, so that by the end we're on the same page about what those words point to: what is Silk Road Dharma, what word am I translating as kinship, and why crossroads?

[We agreed on a shape for the evening: around forty-five minutes of talk, then a guided meditation, then time for questions — with an open invitation to interrupt if I strayed off track or dropped a term in Sanskrit without explaining it.]

To circle back to something Bodhidāsa kindly mentioned: I've had the great privilege of studying with teachers in a number of different traditions, among them the one in which I'm ordained, the Sakya school of Tibetan Buddhism. My home monastery in that tradition is at Boudhanath in Kathmandu — if anyone's been there, through the main gate of the great stūpa, turning left (you only turn right if you're the Bodhanath priest), the first monastery you come to has an enormous Maitreya Buddha — the Buddha-to-be, Buddha of loving-kindness, incidentally. My ordination name, Ngawang Tenzin, is a common one in the Sakya lineage, roughly "power of the voice, holder of the teachings" — Dharma voice, if we shorten it; in Chinese it would be something like *Fǎyīn* (法音), "Dharma voice," though triple-barrelled Dharma names are uncommon there. The way I try to practise with it is to study hard and be careful what I say, because words have great power and the Dharma is vast and profound — I can't promise I'll always get it right, but I hope we find some interesting things to explore this evening.

I've also been lucky enough to train with the late Zen Master Thích Nhất Hạnh at Plum Village in France, and more recently at the beautiful Mountain Spring Monastery in Bilpin, with his disciple Thầy Pháp Hải ("Brother Ocean"), the current abbot there — I know we have some friends here familiar with the Plum Village tradition, and I think the Lotus Buds group still meets in this space. And one of my main teachers, whose name I'll mention again, Thomas Dhammadīpa, who in some ways epitomises Silk Road Dharma for me, also recommended I study in the Theravāda tradition as far as I could — on his advice I travelled to Myanmar and spent time in the Pa-Auk tradition — and through him, and my own practice, I've continued to explore where the meeting place is between traditional *śamatha-vipassanā* (calm abiding and insight, or "stopping and looking deeply" in the Chinese translation) and the Mahāyāna and Yogācāra as found in the Tibetan tradition, in Chán, and in the Vajrayāna. Anything I share that's helpful is thanks to those teachers — the Sakya throne-holder, Thomas Dhammadīpa, and my other teachers; anything confusing or unhelpful is entirely my own creation, certainly not the Buddha's intention, which was to illuminate, not confuse.

The crossroads I'm referring to is on the Silk Road. No doubt there's a Buddha statue somewhere in this space — I wouldn't say it's the clearest example, but even here we can probably see echoes of the aesthetic I'm pointing to: that even now, in an increasingly secular West, one still very much grounded in the Judeo-Christian tradition, the image of Śākyamuni Buddha — the sage of the Śākya clan, seated in meditation, with his particular robes and hairstyle — is instantly recognisable. But one thing we might not know is that this image owes rather more to the Western aesthetic tradition, by way of the Greeks, than we might have guessed. In the centuries immediately following the Buddha's life as a living teacher, there was no iconography at all — no pictures, statues or carvings of Siddhārtha Gautama in any sacred space. At most, an empty chair on the altar, like a throne; an image of a footprint, a wheel, or a stūpa. If anyone's visited Ajaṇṭā or Ellorā in India — there's a classic example in one of the Ajaṇṭā caves, a rock-cut monastery containing a *caitya*, a stūpa carved in stone representing the awakened mind of the Buddha — a dome with a spire, the same shape found throughout Buddhist countries (the Boudhanath stūpa is a particularly enormous and ancient example) — but no picture of the Buddha himself.

Images didn't begin to appear until around the first century BCE, and when they did, they were strongly influenced by Greek sculpture — you can see it in the way the robes are folded, in the wavy ringlets especially in Gandhāran images: the Buddha looks rather like Apollo, like a Greek statue. That's because of how Buddhism was moving along the trade routes at the time, from roughly the second century BCE right up to the tenth century CE, through Gandhāra (present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan), Bactria, Kashmir, Khotan, Kuchā, Dunhuang — caves full of not only Buddha images but texts, at various stages of the transition and translation of the Buddha Dharma into new places, times, systems of value and ways of seeing the world.

This "Apollonian Buddha" arises because that region — Pakistan, Afghanistan — was ground conquered by Alexander the Great and left behind as Greek kingdoms. We have records of Greek philosophers meeting naked ascetics, possibly Jain monks, and exchanging views on the nature of the world. If anyone's been to Swayambhū in Kathmandu, there's a wonderful example there of the contrapposto stance — like Michelangelo's David, weight canted onto one hip, robes folded — in the image of Dīpankara Buddha, a previous Buddha: carved from black basalt rather than white marble, but if you squint, it could almost be a Hellenic statue in negative. The Greek influence is unmistakable.

So when the Buddha Dharma arrived in China, along this Silk Road of exchange and translation, travellers like Xuanzang made the journey — the same Xuanzang behind *Journey to the West*, or *Monkey Magic* if you grew up watching it as I did: the story of a travelling translator-monk journeying to India to bring back scriptures, and not just bring them back, but translate them. In good translation there's always more at work than simply finding the right word — something we as practitioners do every day too, translating an instruction or a text into our own understanding, and then into practice. There are important moments in that transmission: Master Tăng Hội, highly regarded in the Vietnamese tradition, possibly predating the introduction of Chán into China even before Bodhidharma, was the son of a Sogdian merchant father — not ethnically Vietnamese, but a Silk Road Buddhist to the bone.

In this initial phase there's an interesting period of just finding the right word — "matching concepts," *géyì* in Chinese: drawing a kind of matrix, finding a word from the indigenous vocabulary to translate a new one. For us, this is "emptiness" — bearing in mind that for us, that word also carries the sense of zero, a mathematical concept (the North Indian tradition gave us many of our mathematical ideas as well). There's an important process of working out what the word actually means — not just as a word, but in practice. An interesting example: when Buddhism arrived in China, the Taoists were fairly sure Laozi, the author of the *Tao Te Ching*, had returned — in person or through his students — to teach again, because the concepts seemed so tantalisingly similar. In the Taoist understanding, *wú* is the creative unmanifest that precedes the Tao, which precedes yin and yang, the ten thousand things. In a practice as simple as tai chi, one is attempting to relax enough to contact that emptiness behind the movement — otherwise we're just caught in the ten thousand things, the downstream effects. But Sengzhao, among others, was already arguing that this isn't quite emptiness in the Buddhist sense — not quite the same thing. So they had to be careful. In later phases, great teachers like Kumārajīva put real effort into mastering the practice and then properly translating from that understanding, discovering that *kōng* (空) is a better translation of *śūnyatā* than *wú* — *wú* was in fact never really used to translate *śūnyatā*, because emptiness in the Buddhist sense isn't something truly existing from its own side either; emptiness is also empty, so it can't really function as a noun in the same way without getting caught in exactly that trap. In the Heart Sūtra — in Sanskrit, "form is not other than emptiness, emptiness is not other than form" — the Chinese scholars and translators had to stop seeing emptiness through the lens of *wú*, and understand it as a genuinely new concept, not simply matched to a word they already had, but understood to the root through their own practice.

So here we are today, maybe in the middle of a mindfulness revolution — I know some of you have spent years teaching mindfulness in schools. Mindfulness has become a word in popular culture, used in Western psychology as a therapeutic approach. There are other examples of where the Buddha Dharma seems to converge with Western thinking — quantum physics, say, or relativity, which seems to resonate with *paticcasamuppāda*, dependent origination, especially in non-dual Buddhism: at the root of material reality seems to be something ungraspable — you can't really call light a particle or a wave, it's both and neither until you look. Translation really starts to come alive, I think, when we move into this phase: seeing correspondences with what's already embedded in our own time and culture, drawing on a vocabulary from another tradition while being careful not to settle for just one word. Take "mindfulness" — what is that translating? [Someone offered: the character for "mind" in Chinese is really closer to "heart" than "head" — a classic example.] That's a lovely example of this kind of triangulation: not being satisfied with "mindfulness" meaning simply "staying in my mind" — what about "heartfulness"? And again, we might think there's a better word — but actually, the Buddha was doing this constantly: using words already in common use, then explaining what he meant by them. *Sati* is a Vedic word, important because that was a pre-literate, oral tradition — to learn, you first memorised the verse, recited and recalled it (*sati*), and only then learned its meaning. The Buddha took that word and unpacked it differently: yes, it means recollecting — but recollecting what? The wholesome; the four noble truths; the breath; cause and effect, the fact that our actions have consequences, here. This mattered enough to Thich Nhat Hanh that when he devised the seal of Plum Village, he replaced *sīla* with *smṛti* (Sanskrit for *sati*) — mindfulness as the key. It's much more than remembering texts or chanting the Vinaya; it's living by it, remembering in the moment one might otherwise transgress — not a rule followed blindly because it's memorised, but one understood: why is it important not to react in anger, right now. There's an ongoing discussion even in the Tibetan tradition, in Sakya circles especially when speaking with English speakers, about correcting the assumption that "the Buddha has mind" — because "mind" in English is often reserved to translate the conditioned mind, *manas*, not *citta*. A whole different rabbit hole we won't go down tonight — but I'm sure you can all think of words that meant one thing when you first encountered them in study or practice, and turned out to mean something else later. [Someone noted: even "mindfulness of breath" — some translate this more energetically, as *prāṇa*, rather than simply physical breathing.] Indeed — *prāṇa* and *apāna*, the in-breath and up-breath, breath also as energy.

So, as practitioners at a crossroads of time and place, exposed to every doctrine and term imaginable, what do we actually do when we get to the cushion? I'd like to spend the rest of our time on one example of a particularly important practice — one that Thomas Dhammadīpa, that same teacher I mentioned who for me epitomises a Silk Road Buddhist, calls the practice for our times: the cultivation of *mettā*, loving-kindness — an antidote to fear. Canonically, the Buddha offered the instructions on *mettā bhāvanā* in the origin story of the *Mettā Sutta*: a group of monks sent to a forest for the rains retreat found it haunted — headless bodies, bodiless heads, screams, no way to meditate. They went back to the Buddha to complain, and rather than reassigning them to a different grove, he gave them the cultivation of loving-kindness and sent them back. Not just their practice but the forest itself was transformed — the very spirits that had been haunting them became their protectors. I can think of examples from my own life too: a street that's perfectly benign by day can feel transformed into something frightening after watching a scary film the night before — same street, different mind. It's a powerful enough practice to be offered not only as a kind of safeguard between *śamatha* and *vipassanā*, a culminating practice, but also as an initial one — there are examples of the Buddha teaching loving-kindness before breath meditation, as an antidote to anxiety, fear, aversion.

Since we're already in etymology, let's unpack *mettā* a little further. *Mettā*, *maitrī* in Sanskrit, comes from *mitra* — an important Aryan deity of bond and alliance, later more colloquially "friendship." For nomadic Aryan peoples constantly meeting new tribes and neighbours, navigating alliance, accord, even covenant, was very important — important enough that Mitra became a deity to whom offerings were made, to ensure bonds and alliances would hold. *Mettā* also comes to us as one of the *brahmavihāras*, the divine abodes, or immeasurable minds — we'll come back to why "immeasurable" — but the moment we call something divine in our secular age, it tends to float away from daily life. Yet there's an important sense in which *mettā* — and our cultivation of loving-kindness — actually makes us human; it's central to our humanity, along with the other divine abodes: compassion, joy, and equanimity. Thich Nhat Hanh describes all four as aspects of true love.

Where else in our culture, exploring this as Silk Road Buddhists for one evening, can we find reference points for divine love? Jesus of Nazareth — there are lines in the New Testament that pretty much spell it out: God is love. Perhaps *mettā* and *agápē* — the Greek word used in the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the New Testament from Hebrew, for this divine love of God for creation — can help orient us in the cultivation of *mettā bhāvanā*, and perhaps there are ways it's importantly different too, which we need to be careful not to confuse.

[On a question about the Hebrew original:] The word being translated as *agápē* is *ahavah* — already a very important, broad term covering familial love, marital affection, love for one's children, but also covenant: "love your neighbour as yourself," "love the Lord." The Septuagint translators chose not *eros* — the Greeks already had other words for love; Plato's entire *Symposium* is about the different nuances of *eros*, though we've come to use it mostly for attached, erotic, sensual love (in the *mettā bhāvanā* instructions, that kind of attachment is in fact named as the near enemy of *mettā*). They chose *agápē* instead — freer of those implications, better suited to divine love. One of the key differences pointed out later, in the Neoplatonist tradition: desire has a kind of ladder, beginning with sensual love, moving to a love of beauty, then of ideas, and eventually of the abstracted divine — a progression from the worldly to the divine. *Agápē* flows the other way: it's the love of God for creation that brings creation into being in the first place; its divinity lies in that direction of flow, and the more we align with it, the more we align with God. In the Christian understanding, one of the most important ways to align with it is forgiveness — loving someone who, conventionally, doesn't deserve it; giving before it's deserved. That's what God does, on this account, and if we can do the same, we align more closely with *agápē* rather than attached love. [Someone noted the echo of *dāna* here] — exactly: generosity given so freely that it reaches even those we wouldn't conventionally say deserve it. "Whoever does not love does not know God, because God is love."

I'm indebted here to the philosopher and cognitive scientist John Vervaeke, who has an interesting series of talks on some of this: as children, it's a love that allows us to grow into fully independent, well-rounded adults — a creative power that makes us into a person, because our mother, father or caregiver loved us selflessly and unconditionally in our vulnerability, letting us survive that difficult phase. There's something fundamentally creative in that way of thinking about love — it makes us human. And if we turn it around: choosing to love someone rather than hate them lets them become a friend rather than staying in a category of "enemy" or "other" — they become more human to us. There are terrible examples, even now, of the opposite: language used to dehumanise, so that exclusion or worse becomes easier. I think *mettā bhāvanā* is, in an important sense, addressing exactly that tendency — and we'll come back to this, because this is where Silk Road Dharma can draw on contemporary neuroscience to show us what's actually happening, physiologically, in some of these feelings.

So: this word, *mettā*. [It's on the sign outside — quite something, seeing the title of your own talk lit up on an LCD screen on a busy street in Glebe.] I'm proposing we translate *mettā* as "kinship." Why not just "friendliness"? It doesn't quite cover everything we've touched on — divine love, creative love, something transcendent, something a mother has for a child, something that covers even covenant and bond. "Kinship" comes from "kin"/"kind" — the way we behave towards our own kind, our kith and kin, in Anglo-Saxon — which I like, because it returns us to one of the root languages of English, and, interestingly, shares a Proto-Indo-European root with "genesis": a creative implication too, since we have our kin through reproduction, created by that bond.

But what do we learn when we actually start to practise *mettā bhāvanā*? It's not just cultivated towards our kin. [Who remembers the steps?] We start with the self, then someone close by — kin, someone towards whom love flows spontaneously — and only then the neutral one: not normally inside our circle, not a relative, but not someone we fear or feel hatred for either, if such a person comes to mind, perhaps someone we've simply lost touch with. We start with kin, then move into territory with a little more friction — things don't flow as easily, because as humans we get to meet far more people than just our tribe or village, and if we're continually disturbed by ill will towards people outside our immediate circle, it's very difficult to cultivate equanimity or tranquillity. A very concrete, practical reason for the practice.

On to neuroscience: it turns out the same pathway and chemistry that gives us the warm, fuzzy feeling of love face to face with our beloved — oxytocin, the neurotransmitter we're looking for in *mettā bhāvanā* — is also active in identifying "the other." It's present in the angry mob, because wherever there's a tribe, a circle, there's an in and an out. That's not only a bad thing — we're alive because our ancestors both loved their children unconditionally *and* were careful about whom they trusted. But nowadays, through social media, the news, just walking down the street, we meet thousands of people we're not related to — endless opportunities to fall into "us and them," amplified by algorithms that feed us more of whatever already feels like "us" or "them." There are horrific examples of where that amplification leads, up to genocide and mass violence. In *mettā bhāvanā*, the Buddha saw through this trap of othering that comes bundled with kinship and connection — hence the progression from self, to kin, to neutral, to difficult being, breaking the barrier further each time. This is the "immeasurableness" of the immeasurable minds: through practice, we can become adept at giving rise to loving-kindness towards any object at all — not just the cute, cuddly, dear and beloved, but someone we hardly know, or would otherwise mistrust, fear, or reject. An amazing practice for our times, drawing on insight from the Buddha Dharma, from how deeply this idea of transformative love is embedded in the Christian tradition, and from contemporary psychology and neuroscience, to help us understand what we're actually doing when we sit down for *mettā bhāvanā*.

A few questions came up at this point worth mentioning. One: does the object of loving-kindness need to be someone we actually know, or can it be a stranger, someone we've never met? Either is possible, but the instruction is not to pick the most difficult object you can think of — leave the training wheels on for a while; there's no need to spend half an hour re-traumatising yourself with the worst interaction you've had, or the most difficult figure from world history. The aim is someone or something it still feels possible to maintain a felt connection with in the heart space — otherwise the practice becomes merely theoretical, a visualisation only, and oxytocin needs it to stay embodied. One tip, as a preview of the fuller instruction: imagine the difficult person as a child, not in the "uniform" of whatever power or action caused harm, but in a state of vulnerability we can resonate with, the way a mother would with her own child.

Another question: is this the same as empathy? An interesting one — in the Mahāyāna, especially in the Tibetan tradition, loving-kindness and compassion meditation are often hyphenated together, partly because one gives rise to the other (as in *tonglen*, giving-and-taking practice). Empathy is close to the Greek root of compassion in one sense — feeling with, resonating with another's suffering (one Sanskrit synonym for *karuṇā* is literally "to tremble with," to be shaken by another's pain). The difference is that in a Western psychological borrowing of the Greek term, we don't necessarily keep a foothold in the liberating, wisdom side of things — empathy alone, without the wisdom of a way out (not clinging, letting go), is prone to being overwhelmed by worldly grief. That's not to say empathy is negative — only that, to do the work of *karuṇā*, we need the *kar-*, the active, karma-cognate dimension: not just diving into the same suffering, but actively working towards its end. [Someone asked about *tonglen* specifically — breathing in the other's suffering, breathing out freedom and happiness for them.] Yes — combining *mettā* (wishing happiness) with *karuṇā* (connecting with the suffering): not separate acts. I believe in Chinese, "loving-kindness-compassion" is often rendered as a single double-character term, reflecting how closely bound the two are.

I'm feeling contented with what's been shared so far — contented enough for now.
