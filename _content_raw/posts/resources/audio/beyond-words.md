It's almost a cliché that skilled meditation means "emptying your mind." This talk, for the Metta Centre at UNSW Parramatta, takes that cliché apart: the etymology of *jhāna* itself (which gives us *dhyāna*, *Chán* and *Zen*) means "to think" — so what, when, and how are we actually invited to think in the cultivation of calm and insight? Covers vitarka-vicāra and the bee-and-flower simile from the *Visuddhimagga*, the Buddha's "I"-propositions in MN 2, the subject/object split (Descartes' wax and his "automata" in hats and coats, by way of Iain McGilchrist), and why an AI — all pattern-matching, no capacity to feel — is the reductio of a mind with no path laid on top of its own store of conditioning. Closes with a guided sit.

*[Opening chant: the threefold homage, Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa.]*

A very warm welcome once again, dear friends. Thanks again for the opportunity to share some thoughts and reflections on this topic, which I had a lot of fun preparing — maybe a little too much fun; you might have to stop me if we go too far into the weeds, because I'm a fan of etymology and the origins of words we find ourselves practising with — even "meditation" itself: what does this word actually mean, what are we supposed to be doing? And there's a slight irony in speaking at length about a topic that begins with the phrase "beyond words" — so what kind of words can lead us beyond words? We'll explore some perspectives from the Western philosophical tradition too, which I think both help us understand this, and have been a source of real confusion about how thinking and meditation relate.

First, I'd like to acknowledge the country we're seated on here — the Burramattagal clan of the Dharug people, on whose land the University of Western Sydney's Parramatta campus sits. At Gaia Forest, where I live on Gumbaynggirr country, we're very inspired by the heritage of stewardship of country that we've been fortunate to have access to, if we pay mind to it, from the traditional owners — and I find it genuinely inspiring that there's a sense of open-heartedness and connection to country there that in an important way goes beyond words. Maybe in some small way, our own practice can also help connect us to place, wherever we live or come from.

I'd like to begin with a question for the room, and for everyone on Zoom: did you notice thinking, when we sat together just now, briefly, at the beginning? Very relaxed, calm, an empty mind — raise a hand if that was your experience. A few hands. Did anyone notice thinking instead — words, narratives, a voice, a kind of internal conversation? Some hands, including one very honest "same, same." Related to the meditation, or not? Unrelated, mostly. So it's almost axiomatic, almost a cliché, that meditation somehow involves emptying our minds. There's the very famous Bruce Lee line — "empty your mind, be formless, shapeless, like water; you put water into a cup, it becomes the cup" — this is the early '70s, one of the first popular images of an Asian martial art bringing this kind of wisdom to the West. Or there's *Kung Fu Panda* — the scene where Po repeats "inner peace, inner peace" while visibly failing to achieve any such thing.

So we have this image that a meditator sits and is somehow able to make the mind go completely blank — maybe especially so in daily life, when the mind is very busy, we wish for that kind of rest from continuous thinking. This was certainly my own idea when I first started learning to meditate, particularly through an early interest in the Zen tradition. There's the origin story of the first patriarch of Zen — not found in the suttas, but the story goes that the Buddha gave a teaching by simply holding up a flower and saying nothing; of all the venerable ones present, only Mahākāśyapa smiled, and the Buddha knew he had understood this silent teaching. For the Zen tradition, that's the beginning of a wordless transmission of Dharma from teacher to disciple — an insight beyond words, directly transmitted.

So where does the word "Zen" actually come from? From the Chinese, *Chán*. And *Chán* comes from the Sanskrit *dhyāna* — which is also the word we're translating as "meditation." We borrowed "meditation" itself from the Catholic contemplative tradition, the Western scholastic tradition — we'll come back to that. But in Pāli and Sanskrit, *dhyāna*/*jhāna* implies deep tranquillity — it's the content of *śamatha*, calm-abiding meditation: the four *jhānas* of subtle form, and the four formless attainments beyond them (which aren't canonically counted as *jhāna* — they're *samāpatti*). The four *jhānas* are the ones that matter for us today. Depending on which teacher you follow, these may or may not sit at the centre of your own practice — some traditions combine *śamatha* and *vipassanā* from the beginning; others insist that cultivating insight requires first mastering the ability to enter and exit, at will, this very purified, continuous absorption in a single object, a mind that's very still.

I won't get us bogged down in the technicalities of the factors of entry into the first *jhāna*, but it matters to know that certain mental qualities let the mind move from jumping, uninvited, from object to object — because that's usually our experience: a thought pops up and we follow it, whether we like it or not, pleasant or (often) unpleasant. Ever notice how hard it is not to read a billboard when you're driving? Apparently it's not just me who's tried not to. Try it: it happens automatically — attention is drawn to an object, and straight away we're engaged with it, following the story. The Buddha identified and named exactly this: *vitarka* and *vicāra*, two mental factors present in the first *jhāna*. The interesting thing is that to enter deeper absorption, the instruction is to notice that they're actually a little inconvenient — not very restful — and to let them go, allowing the mind to settle more deeply still.

The etymology of *vitarka* relates to logic — Pāli *takka* implies reasoning, consideration. The *Visuddhimagga*, the "Path of Purification" — a hugely important Theravāda meditation manual that many forest traditions, Burmese and Thai, follow closely — gives us Buddhaghosa's image: *vitarka* is like a bee launching itself at a flower, and *vicāra* is the bee, having found the flower, now circling it, looking for the pollen. Just as the mind goes to an idea — or a word in a sentence — and then stays with it, chewing over it. This is so bound up with language that it's hard to imagine a train of thought without a mind that can move to an idea and then stay with it.

This tendency — *vitarka* running on and on — is rumination, and it happens to be exactly the temperament for which *ānāpānasati*, mindfulness of breathing, is prescribed as the best practice. We know from Western psychology that overthinking relates to depression and anxiety: depression tends to stay with a negative story about oneself, to the exclusion of all other possibilities, worst-case only; anxiety is the same tendency mirrored into the future — that's not going to work, it's not going to be possible. These aren't necessarily bad thoughts in themselves — it's good to have checks and balances, a "hmm, that was risky" — the problem is when we keep thinking about it long after it should have taken its proportionate place in the rest of reality: yes, I shouldn't have said that, but that was three years ago, I can let it go now. We lose that sense of proportion. This is rumination — the same word we use for cows, which take in grass, then sit in the shade and chew it again and again. The cure appears again and again, in the Yogācāra and Mahāyāna traditions but also right in the Pāli discourses: *ānāpānasati*, calming *vitarka* by easing back on the thinking mind. There's a precise sequence for this tied to the twelve links of dependent origination, *paṭiccasamuppāda*: contact gives rise to feeling, feeling to perception, perception to thinking (*vitarka*), and thinking to *papañca* — proliferation, the piling of story on story, which the sutta itself says then "assails" a person with notions of past, present and future. Rumination — overthinking, in other words.

So it seems fairly clear that the "empty your mind" image is partly true — but also that to get there, we first need to know how to think skilfully. If you just empty your mind without that skill, not much happens — or, as someone rightly observed, it can get worse: an undirected arrow. The same discourse that gives us the progression through the *jhānas* also gives us right effort (*sammā-vāyāma*) as four jobs for the thinking mind: preventing unskilful states not yet arisen; letting go of ones that have; giving rise to skilful states not yet arisen; sustaining the skilful states already present. "There is the case where a monk, quite secluded from sensuality, secluded from unskilful states, enters and remains in the first *jhāna*: rapture and pleasure born of seclusion, accompanied by *vitarka* and *vicāra*" — applied thinking of the right, wholesome kind, applied to the breath: I'm aware that I'm breathing in, thinking about the fact that I'm breathing in, not about pizza, or a conversation from last month. "With the stilling of *vitarka* and *vicāra*, one enters the second *jhāna*" — rapture and pleasure born of concentration, unification of awareness, free of *vitarka* and *vicāra*: the bee has landed, no longer zooming after the flower or buzzing around it.

For our purposes, the first two stages matter most: thinking applied in the right way, and then noticing that even that right application gently fades into the background — because we've already found the object we wanted to stay with, so we don't need to keep thinking about it in the ordinary sense, that mind always looking for the next idea. Does anyone remember playing the childhood game of repeating a single word until it stops meaning anything? Apparently it's not just me and the billboards. Try it sometime: say any word again and again, and the beginning and end stop making sense — the word breaks apart into mere sound. There's a term for this in cognitive science, semantic satiation: we become saturated with the meaning, and start to notice it's really just shapes our lips and tongue are making — not the bee, or the flower.

Much of what I'm sharing today draws on a lovely essay, "Thinking about Jhāna," by Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu — I'll share a link afterwards for anyone interested. The questions that are so fatiguing are the questions about "I" — was I in the past, shall I be in the future, am I, am I not, what am I. The Buddha recommended these not as false or true exactly, but as a kind of fetter, a hindrance to happiness — a real pain in the neck. Contrast that with the language of the four noble truths: no "I" anywhere. This is suffering; this is the cause of suffering; this is the cessation of suffering; this is the path. Not "why am I suffering, how do I get out of it" — no subject, no object, just looking directly at what's happening. It's striking that this is how the Buddha, as master physician of awakening, chose to frame his diagnosis.

I did promise a brief dive into Western philosophy here — I won't dwell too long, because I'd like plenty of time for questions and for an actual sit. What's actually happening with "I" propositions? "I like this bell," "I feel happy," "I want to go to the park" — even "I am breathing in, I am aware of my in-breath." Compare that with simply: in-breath, out-breath; breathing in, breathing out. In the first, there's an "I" and "my breath" — a subject and an object. In the second, there's just attention on the process, the feeling, what's actually happening. That subject-object split is doing its work under the hood of nearly all our inner narrative: am I running late, where are my keys, and so on. What if, instead, we simply notice rushing, or worrying, or thinking — what's actually happening in this moment, with no "I" and no "my"?

This is worth noticing, because the Latin itself encodes the split: *object* literally means "that which is thrown against"; *subject*, "that which is thrown under." Which makes it a little ironic that we have the idiom "thrown under the bus" — because that's rather what we're doing to ourselves whenever we're not aware of how readily we tangle ourselves up this way, in the chain of fetters, in dependent origination. *Bhava*, "becoming," is exactly this: the moment we turn the subject into an object, make a story about "me" and "the problem," and in a sense throw ourselves under the bus — because the bus is the accumulated weight of all that past thought and story, which has a real inertia, very difficult to slow down once it's rolling, and which can itself be a real source of anxiety and depression.

Which brings us to Descartes, who — fairly or not — gets blamed for a lot of the problems of Western dualistic thinking. (I once visited his tomb, in a small church in Paris, and lit a candle for him, because he takes so much of the blame for what people call the modern meaning crisis.) He's famous for *cogito ergo sum* — "I think, therefore I am" — written, with no small irony, in a work he called his *Meditations*. Which is no wonder we find the words "meditation" and "thinking" so tangled together in the West: *meditatio* was simply the nearest Latin word available to scholars trying to translate *dhyāna* — "sitting still, some mental process going on," real but not the whole story, because in the beginning we do need to think, in just the way we've been exploring — we just also need to know when to let that go, and simply be with the breathing.

Descartes has another, more famous image: the wax argument. He takes a piece of fresh honeycomb — the smell of flowers, the sweetness, the drip, everything that stimulates the senses — and holds it to a flame. It melts: no smell, no taste, no shape, nothing left of what the senses first reported — and yet, he says, he still knows it's the same wax. So he concludes that what's reliable is the thought, the judgement, "this is wax" — not the senses, which can't be trusted, because this marvellous body of sensation is changing all the time. The only thing he can really trust is his own thinking. And then something sadder happens: a paragraph later, he looks out his window at people in hats and coats crossing the square, and realises that — on his own logic — he can't actually be sure there are real human beings with real inner lives underneath those hats and coats; he can only infer it. Thinking alone has become more real to him than the world. For some philosophers, this was the start of a very deep rabbit hole in Western thought — we really did throw ourselves under the bus, making subject and object, trusting only thinking over sensation and feeling. (The Romantic movement was in part a reaction against exactly this — a conversation for another time.)

There does seem to be something like two ways of seeing the world, and — leaning now on Western neuroscience and psychology — it seems the left hemisphere is very good at exactly this subject-object splitting; it's where our language lives, where *vitarka* and *vicāra* do their work. The right hemisphere is more associated with art, music, the capacity to handle flow and change and to see the whole, rather than splitting things into separate parts to be examined like machinery. This matters for how we relate to one another, and maybe especially as ecologists, for how we relate to the world — because if we cut ourselves out of it, treat ourselves as just one more part in a machine, we lose the very thing that keeps the planet, and therefore us, alive: interdependence, relationship, flow and change. (It's really more nuanced than a strict left/right split — more about front and back of the brain too — happy to share references for anyone who wants to go deeper.)

It's worth noticing that this same moment in history — the rise of literacy, of written language — is right around when a whole cluster of spiritual teachers turned up to grapple with exactly this: the Buddha, the Greek philosophers, the Hebrew prophets, all finding ways to deal with this new wave of abstracted, subject-object thinking, as everyone suddenly learned to read and acquired an internal narrative. Worth exploring another time.

So: what current technology is really built on this same subject-object split in language? From the room: AI. AI runs on large language models — words and text taken from across the internet into a vast library an algorithm can search and recombine into meaningful sentences. We've taught computers our language, rather than learning theirs, so that we can speak to each other in plain English, or Chinese, or French.

Before going further into that, it's worth hearing the classical similes for the *jhānas*, because they show why feeling matters so much here, and connect directly to where we're headed with AI. A bath attendant kneading soap powder into water until it's completely diffused — no part of the soap untouched by the water, no part of the water untouched by the soap, no dryness left, all of it infused with moisture — the first *jhāna*, pleasant sensation unified with the feeling of the breath penetrating the whole body: feeling, not thinking about the breath. A lake fed by its own spring from beneath, with no need for any inflow or outflow — the second *jhāna*, no further need for *vitarka* and *vicāra*, already calm. A lotus flourishing fully in the water, no part of it untouched — the third. And a person sitting wrapped completely in white cloth, no part of the body left uncovered, this radiance infusing the whole body — the fourth: feeling, *vedanā*, not thinking, in this context of absorption.

We ask AI a lot of questions these days — plenty of us have googled a question about the Dharma; apparently I'm not the only one. The Buddha himself used a great many words, so it's genuinely handy to have ways to navigate that enormous library. But notice what a large language model does very well, and what it cannot do at all. It does *vitarka*-*vicāra* extremely well — makes excellent grammatical sentences, splits the world into pieces, makes connections, builds narratives; you could ask it to write you a novel and publish it tomorrow. What it cannot do: pay attention, or feel the breath in a body — because there's no body there to breathe with, nothing there to feel with. Something important seems to be missing from any idea of intelligence, artificial or otherwise, that works only with language, only with the subject-object split: it can answer a great many questions, but we'll need to be careful it doesn't throw us under the bus in turn, because what we also need is the capacity to feel — to be absorbed enough in feeling to actually see how inconvenient, and sometimes inappropriate, our thinking can be when it proliferates, caught in the past or running into the future, the very thing that causes so much anxiety and depression. *Vedanā* — feeling — turns out to be the key link in the chain: after contact comes feeling, and after feeling, perception; that's exactly where the chain can be broken.

I'd like to stop there, to leave enough time for questions and discussion, and enough time to actually sit and put some of this into practice.

Alfin asked about the relationship between meditation, contemplation, and the deeper stages the talk had described — are they all related? A good question. The three kinds of wisdom are relevant here: *sutamayā paññā*, wisdom from hearing the instructions — this is me, I'm breathing in, there's subject and object; *cintāmayā paññā*, wisdom from reflecting — closer to what we mean today by "contemplation" (English actually inherited two separate Latin options, *meditatio* and *contemplatio* — meditation leaning more to the thinking end, contemplation more to the absorptive end, though we've rather flipped the sense of the words around since); this is where we question and check we've understood correctly, rather than following some beautiful swirling purple light that isn't actually the object we were given. And *bhāvanāmayā paññā*, wisdom from cultivation — putting the instructions into practice, because that's the only way the fruit of the path is ever realised, not from thinking about the Dharma, or even from thoroughly resolving every question about it, but from tending it, like a good gardener. So: yes, we need to think and be clear, and we also need, eventually, to let go of even that thinking, becoming familiar with the object simply as feeling, not as something separate from body and mind.

Maya asked, as someone who's spent years moving between different Buddhist traditions and practices, how to think about combining different techniques, and about the sense she'd heard that mixing traditions can be a bit fraught — given there's no one-size-fits-all, and Sydney in particular can feel like a supermarket, or an online shopping mall, of Buddhism. My own sense is that the solution is contained in those same three wisdoms — many wells dug shallowly do not reach water, and I've been most reassured by teachers who've emphasised that, while we need to dig a well deeply enough to actually taste the water, the water itself is the same water. Once we've tasted that directly, through cultivation, we can recognise the same essence even when it's expressed in very different words, across different traditions and schools — using that as a skilful means, rather than concluding "that's wrong, I'm right" or "I was wrong, I need to switch." Different skilful means, for different times, places and people, even within the Buddha's own teaching career — not one solution for everyone. There's a real pressure in our culture to build a narrowly defined identity in order to promote and differentiate "your" path from the crowd — "this is the way" — but set against how many ways of being there are in the world, let alone individuals, that's a highway to nowhere. The Buddha's often-misquoted invitation — "don't take my word for it" — isn't an invitation to simply form your own opinion; it's an invitation to actually have a go, to put it into practice, and find out for yourself, because the Dharma is there to be tasted if we apply ourselves. And whether or not a given tradition emphasises *jhāna* by name, or combines *śamatha* and *vipassanā* from the start, or the reverse — universally, the Buddha and the teachers since have been inviting us to see the world differently: to notice how subtly and persistently we split experience into subject and object whenever we're caught only in *vitarka*-*vicāra*, and to see, instead, relationship, interdependence, nothing existing separately from its own side. When that shift happens — whatever we call it — there's no "I," no becoming, no *bhava*, among the links; we've cut the fetters.

Mel then asked a practice question: given that the *Ānāpānasati Sutta* already includes both *vipassanā* and *śamatha* elements, and that it's recommended for a ruminating mind, how do you actually practise with a sutta that's — in her words — so wordy, when what you're often reaching for is one-pointed attention on the breath? Doesn't inserting all that language — "gladdening the mind" and so on — risk becoming disruptive to the sitting itself? A beautiful question — let's just do it: let's meditate, and I'll try to answer it in the practice itself.
